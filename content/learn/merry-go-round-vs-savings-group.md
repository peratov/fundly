Informal savings groups come in two basic designs. Knowing which one you're running — or should be running — saves a lot of confusion.

## The rotating model (ROSCA)

A **rotating savings and credit association** is what most people call a [merry-go-round](/learn/what-is-a-merry-go-round), susu or rotating chama. Everyone pays in each round; one person takes the whole pot; after everyone has had a turn, the circle ends.

- Money is **never kept** by the group — it passes straight through.
- Nobody earns interest. Everyone receives exactly what they paid in.
- It's simple, fast and needs almost no bookkeeping.

## The accumulating model (ASCA)

An **accumulating savings and credit association** keeps members' savings in a **shared fund** that grows. Members can borrow from it and repay with interest, and the interest is shared among members — usually in proportion to how much they've saved. Village savings and loan associations (VSLAs), SACCO-style groups, alumni welfare funds and investment chamas all follow this model.

- The group **holds money** over time, so it needs records, rules and oversight.
- Savers **earn a return** from the interest borrowers pay.
- Members can borrow **what they need, when they need it** — not only on their turn.

## Side by side

| | Merry-go-round (ROSCA) | Savings group (ASCA) |
|---|---|---|
| Where money goes | Straight to one member each round | Into a shared fund |
| Returns | None — you get back what you paid | Interest from loans shared with savers |
| Borrowing | Only "on your turn" | Anytime, subject to rules |
| Welfare fund | Not usually | Common (funerals, medical, emergencies) |
| Records needed | Who paid, who's next | Savings, loans, repayments, shares, welfare |
| Main risk | Early recipient stops paying | Poor lending decisions, weak controls |
| Governance | An organiser | Officials, committees, audits |

## When to switch from a merry-go-round

Consider moving to (or adding) an accumulating fund when:

- members keep asking to **borrow outside their turn**;
- the group wants to **support members** through funerals, illness or school fees;
- you'd like savings to **earn something** rather than just rotate;
- the group has grown beyond people who all know each other personally.

The trade-off is more responsibility: you'll need a [constitution](/learn/group-fund-constitution-template), [clear roles](/learn/treasurer-roles-and-committees), [lending rules](/learn/how-to-run-loans-in-a-savings-group) and proper records — ideally [double-entry books](/learn/double-entry-bookkeeping-for-savings-groups) so the numbers always reconcile.

## Doing both

Many groups keep the fun and discipline of a rotation while building a shared fund: for example, of a GHS 300 monthly contribution, GHS 200 rotates and GHS 100 goes into a fund for loans and welfare.

## A decision guide

Answer these five questions:

1. Do members mainly want a **lump sum at a predictable time**? → Merry-go-round.
2. Do members want to **borrow when they need to**, not when their turn comes? → Savings group.
3. Does the group want to **support members in emergencies**? → Savings group with a welfare fund.
4. Is anyone willing to **keep proper records** every month? → Needed for a savings group.
5. Do members **know each other well**? → Either works; if not, start with a small merry-go-round to build trust.

Many groups start as a merry-go-round and add a savings fund after a year or two, once trust and record-keeping habits are established.
