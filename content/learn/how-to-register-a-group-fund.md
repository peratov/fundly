Most savings and welfare groups start informally, with a WhatsApp group, a treasurer and a notebook. That's fine at first. But as the fund grows, questions come up. Can we open a bank account? Who is liable if something goes wrong? What happens to the money if the treasurer dies? Registration is one answer. This guide explains the common options and their trade-offs. It is general information, not legal advice.

## Signs it's time to think about registration

- the fund holds more than a few months of members' income;
- you want a bank account that isn't in a member's name;
- you plan to lend to members at interest;
- you want to receive grants, donations or sponsorship;
- you have 50+ members, or members abroad (see [diaspora members](/learn/diaspora-members-group-savings));
- a dispute has shown that nobody knows who legally owns the money.

## Option 1: Informal group with a written constitution

**What it is:** no registration, but a signed constitution, minutes appointing officers, and clear records.

**Good for:** small funds, merry-go-rounds, new groups.

**Trade-offs:** the group usually has no separate legal identity. Officers may be personally exposed, and some banks won't open an account. The constitution still does most of the governance work, so start with our [group fund constitution template](/learn/group-fund-constitution-template) whatever you choose later.

## Option 2: Registered association, society or self-help group

Many countries let community groups register with a social-welfare or community-development office for a modest fee. Kenya's self-help group registration is a well-known example. Other countries use societies or associations registries.

**Good for:** welfare funds, alumni associations and chamas that mainly serve members.

**Trade-offs:** you'll usually file annual returns and hold an AGM (see [how to run an AGM](/learn/how-to-run-an-agm)). The benefit is recognition by banks and a clearer status.

## Option 3: Cooperative, credit union or SACCO

A savings and credit cooperative is owned by its members and built for savings and lending. In some countries, cooperatives that take deposits above certain thresholds are supervised by a financial regulator (Kenya's SASRA for deposit-taking SACCOs, for example).

**Good for:** groups whose main activity is savings and member loans, and which expect to grow large.

**Trade-offs:** more rules (minimum members, audited accounts, capital and liquidity requirements) and more cost. In return you get strong legitimacy and access to the wider cooperative movement.

## Option 4: Company limited by guarantee or incorporated trustees

Some alumni associations register as a non-profit company limited by guarantee, or as incorporated trustees (the route used in Nigeria through the Corporate Affairs Commission).

**Good for:** alumni associations with charitable activities such as scholarships or projects, that want a durable legal identity.

**Trade-offs:** company filings, and sometimes a requirement for audited accounts. It usually doesn't suit an active lending fund without further permissions.

## How to decide

| Question | If yes, lean towards |
|---|---|
| Mostly welfare and dues, fewer than 200 members? | Informal or registered association |
| Lending is the core activity and you'll grow? | Cooperative / SACCO |
| Charitable projects, donations, scholarships? | Company limited by guarantee / trustees |
| Just a rotating circle of friends? | Stay informal with written rules |

## What to do regardless of the legal form

Registration doesn't make a fund well run; good habits do. Whatever route you choose:

- open a dedicated account with dual signatories (see [opening a group bank account](/learn/opening-a-group-bank-account));
- separate the roles of treasurer, secretary and chair (see [treasurer roles and committees](/learn/treasurer-roles-and-committees));
- keep double-entry records and reconcile monthly;
- publish a simple report to members every month;
- audit once a year using our [annual audit checklist](/learn/annual-audit-checklist-savings-group).

## Next steps

Contact the relevant registrar in your country and ask for its current requirements and fees. Then take a written proposal to your members, ideally at an AGM, because a change of legal form is a decision the whole membership should make.
