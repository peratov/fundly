A merry-go-round is the simplest group savings arrangement there is. Everyone pays the same amount on a schedule, and each round one member takes the whole pot. If you are new to the idea, read [what a merry-go-round is](/learn/what-is-a-merry-go-round) first. This guide is the practical launch plan.

## Step 1: Choose people, not numbers

The success of a circle depends almost entirely on who is in it. Before thinking about amounts, list people who meet three tests:

- **You would lend them this amount on your own.** If you wouldn't, don't put them in the circle.
- **They have a steady income on the schedule you'll use.** Someone paid monthly will struggle with a weekly circle.
- **They will say early if they are in trouble.** Honesty matters more than wealth.

It's fine to start small. A reliable circle of six is much better than a shaky circle of fifteen. You can open a second round to more people once the first one finishes cleanly.

## Step 2: Set an amount everyone can pay in a bad month

Pick the contribution that the *least* comfortable member can pay in their worst month, not the one the most comfortable member suggests. Then check that the pot is useful: with 8 members at GHS 300 a month, each person receives GHS 2,400 once. Is that enough for what people want it for, such as rent, school fees or stock for a business? If not, add a member or a little more time rather than raising the amount beyond what's safe.

If you can't decide between weekly and monthly rounds, our comparison of [weekly vs monthly merry-go-rounds](/learn/weekly-vs-monthly-merry-go-round) covers the trade-offs.

## Step 3: Agree the payout order openly

Order is where most arguments start. Receiving early is like getting an interest-free loan, and receiving late is like saving at zero interest, as explained in [is a merry-go-round worth it?](/learn/is-a-merry-go-round-worth-it). So agree the method before anyone knows their position:

- a random draw everyone can see;
- priority for a member with a known, dated need (like school fees in January);
- or a mix of the two.

Our guide to [choosing a fair payout order](/learn/merry-go-round-payout-order) walks through each option. The [free merry-go-round planner](/tools/merry-go-round) can spin a fair, random order and share the schedule with one link.

## Step 4: Write down the rules

Keep it to one page. At minimum, cover:

1. the amount, the due day and how to pay;
2. the payout order and how swaps are allowed;
3. what happens if someone pays late;
4. what happens if someone wants to leave;
5. who keeps the record.

You can copy and adapt our [merry-go-round rules template](/learn/merry-go-round-rules-template). Share it in the group chat and ask every member to reply "agreed", so you have a record.

## Step 5: Run the first meeting well

The first round sets the tone. Use this checklist:

- confirm everyone has the schedule with dates and names;
- confirm the payment method, whether mobile money to the recipient or to a group wallet;
- agree how people will show proof of payment;
- set a reminder routine; our [WhatsApp reminder templates](/learn/whatsapp-reminder-templates-for-dues) save time here;
- make the first payout on time, even if the organiser has to chase people.

## Step 6: Protect the circle from the start

Most failures come from one member who takes an early payout and stops paying. Two simple safeguards help a lot:

- put members who are newer to the group **later** in the order;
- ask members who receive early to keep paying *before* anyone else's payout is due, not after.

If a member does fall behind, handle it quickly and privately. See [what to do when a member doesn't pay](/learn/when-a-member-doesnt-pay). Also learn to spot schemes that only *look* like merry-go-rounds; our guide to [merry-go-round scams and warning signs](/learn/merry-go-round-scams-warning-signs) explains the difference.

## When the circle ends

When the last person has been paid, close the round formally. Post a short summary of who paid and who received, and thank people. Then decide together whether to run again, change the amount, or add new members. Many long-running savings groups began as one small circle of friends that simply kept going.
