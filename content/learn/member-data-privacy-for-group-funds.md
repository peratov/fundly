Group funds hold some of the most sensitive information people have: phone numbers, ID numbers, next of kin, income, debts and sometimes health or bereavement details. Members trust officers with it. Handling it carefully is part of running a trustworthy fund, and in many countries it's also a legal duty. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

## Collect only what you need

| Data | Why you need it |
|---|---|
| Name, phone, email | Contact, reminders, receipts |
| Member number | Matching payments |
| Paying phone numbers | [Reconciliation](/learn/reconciling-mobile-money-statements) |
| Nominee / next of kin | Paying benefits if a member dies |
| ID number | Only if a bank, registrar or loan agreement requires it |
| Income estimate | Only for loan applications |

Don't collect things "just in case", like full date of birth, home address or employer, unless you have a real use for them.

## Limit who can see what

- **Members** should see their own records, plus fund totals.
- **The treasurer and loan committee** see what they need for their role.
- **Everyone else** sees totals only.

Paper registers passed around at meetings and spreadsheets emailed to the whole committee are the most common leaks. Software with role-based access, where each officer only sees what their role needs, fixes this. See [moving from WhatsApp and Excel](/learn/moving-from-whatsapp-and-excel).

## The WhatsApp problem

WhatsApp groups are brilliant for community, but they're a poor place for personal financial data:

- every member sees every message, forever, on their own phone;
- messages get forwarded outside the group;
- members' phone numbers are visible to everyone in the group.

Good practice:

- post **totals**, not lists of debtors (see the [monthly financial report](/learn/monthly-financial-report-for-members));
- send arrears reminders **privately**; our [WhatsApp reminder templates](/learn/whatsapp-reminder-templates-for-dues) include private versions;
- never post ID numbers, bank details or health details;
- for welfare events, share only what the family agrees to.

## Keep records secure

- protect phones and laptops with a PIN or password;
- use a shared, access-controlled system rather than personal files;
- keep backups, so losing one phone doesn't mean losing the fund's history;
- remove access promptly when an officer steps down;
- lock paper records away and don't take them home long term.

## Tell members what you do with their data

Put a short privacy note in your membership form or [constitution](/learn/group-fund-constitution-template):

> We collect your contact details, nominee and payment records to run the fund. Officers see only what their role needs. We share fund totals, not individual details, with members. You can ask to see or correct your data at any time.

## Know the law in your country

Many countries now have data protection laws, often with a regulator you can contact for guidance. Examples include Ghana's Data Protection Act 2012, Kenya's Data Protection Act 2019 and Nigeria's Data Protection Act 2023. Obligations can include keeping data secure, collecting only what's needed, letting people access their data and, in some cases, registering with the regulator. Check the rules that apply to your group, especially if you have [members abroad](/learn/diaspora-members-group-savings).

## Privacy from day one

The best time to set good habits is when a group starts. If you're setting up a new circle, build these practices in alongside the basics in [how to start a merry-go-round with friends](/learn/how-to-start-a-merry-go-round-with-friends). And when members leave, delete or archive their personal details according to your [member joining and exit policy](/learn/member-joining-and-exit-policy).
