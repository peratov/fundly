**Esusu** and **ajo** are among the oldest financial institutions in West Africa, with roots in Yoruba communities long before formal banks arrived. Today they're used by market traders, artisans, office workers and diaspora communities alike.

## Two models you'll come across

### Rotating esusu

A group agrees a fixed contribution and frequency. Each round, one member receives the whole pot, until everyone has had a turn. It's the same idea as a [merry-go-round](/learn/what-is-a-merry-go-round): no interest, no fees, and early recipients effectively get an interest-free advance from the group.

### Ajo with an alajo (collector)

An *alajo* collects a fixed daily amount from each saver — often traders — and returns the total at the end of the month, usually keeping one day's contribution as a fee. It's a savings service more than a group, and it's only as safe as the collector.

## Why esusu still matters

- It works for people with irregular daily income.
- It builds discipline through community commitment.
- It gives access to a lump sum without a bank loan.
- It's flexible — groups set their own amounts, schedules and rules.

## Running a trustworthy esusu today

1. **Know your members.** Esusu works because members are accountable to each other. Keep circles among people with real-world connections.
2. **Agree the order openly.** A recorded random draw prevents accusations of favouritism — see [deciding the payout order](/learn/merry-go-round-payout-order).
3. **Use traceable payments.** Bank transfers or mobile money leave a record; cash handed to one person doesn't.
4. **Write the rules down.** Contribution amount, due time, late penalty, what happens on default. Copy our [rules template](/learn/merry-go-round-rules-template).
5. **Keep one shared record** of who has paid each round and who is next.

## Red flags

> If someone invites you to an "esusu" where you pay once and receive back several times more, it is not esusu. Real rotating savings circles only ever return what members put in.

Also be careful of any circle where the organiser insists on holding all the money, won't share records, or keeps changing the payout order.

## From esusu to a group fund

When your circle wants to keep savings together, lend to members at an agreed rate or build a welfare fund, you've outgrown a pure rotation. Our guide on [merry-go-rounds vs savings groups](/learn/merry-go-round-vs-savings-group) explains the difference.

## Rotating esusu vs collector ajo at a glance

| | Rotating esusu | Collector ajo |
|---|---|---|
| Who holds the money | Passed straight to the recipient | The alajo, until the cycle ends |
| Fee | None | Usually one contribution per cycle |
| Access to a lump sum | On your turn | At the end of each cycle |
| Main risk | A member stops paying after their turn | The collector disappears |
| Best for | Friends, colleagues, associations | Traders saving small daily amounts |

## Questions to ask before joining any circle

1. Who organises it, and how long have they done it?
2. Is the payout order written down and shared?
3. Where does the money sit between collection and payout?
4. What happens if someone stops paying?
5. Can I see a record of who has paid each round?

If the answers are vague, walk away — a genuine circle has nothing to hide.
