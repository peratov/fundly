Even well-run funds have loans that fall behind. What separates healthy funds is a **predictable process** applied the same way to everyone. Here's one that works.

## Stage 0: Prevent

- Share the **full repayment schedule** at approval.
- Send a reminder a few days before each due date — [WhatsApp templates here](/learn/whatsapp-reminder-templates-for-dues).
- Let members repay by [mobile money](/learn/collecting-dues-with-mobile-money) from anywhere.

## Stage 1: Due date missed — friendly contact

Within a few days, a private message: *"Hi Peace, your loan instalment of GHS 383 was due on the 15th. Is everything OK?"* Many late payments are forgotten, not refused.

## Stage 2: Overdue status

If the scheduled amount hasn't been paid, mark the loan **overdue**. This is a factual status, not a punishment: it tells the committee to pay attention, and it typically affects the member's [credit score](/learn/fair-credit-scoring-for-members) and ability to take new loans.

The simplest way to judge "overdue" is to compare what should have been paid by today, according to the schedule, with what has actually been paid. Any shortfall is **arrears**.

## Stage 3: Apply the agreed penalty

Apply the penalty written in your lending policy — for example, a fixed amount per missed instalment. Record it on the loan with a reason. Good practice:

- Payments go to **penalties first**, then interest and principal, so the arrears don't keep growing silently.
- When the member catches up, the loan returns to **active** status.

## Stage 4: Talk about restructuring

If a member's circumstances have genuinely changed (job loss, illness), a structured plan is better for everyone than a default: extend the term, reduce instalments temporarily, or pause penalties. Record any agreement.

## Stage 5: Default and recovery

If there's no payment and no engagement after the period in your policy, mark the loan **defaulted**. Then:

- call on any **guarantor**;
- offset against the member's **savings** if your constitution allows it;
- agree a recovery plan.

## Stage 6: Write-off (last resort)

If recovery fails, the committee may **write off** the remaining principal. In the books, this moves the unpaid amount from *loans receivable* to a *write-off expense*, so members' reports stay honest. Recovery can still continue afterwards.

## Principles that keep it fair

- The same process for every member, **including officials**.
- Decisions recorded with reasons — the audit committee will check. See the [annual audit checklist](/learn/annual-audit-checklist-savings-group).
- Private conversations first, never public shaming.

## A worked example

Peace borrowed GHS 4,000 over 12 months at 15% flat: interest GHS 600, instalments of about GHS 383. After four months she has paid one instalment.

- **Scheduled by now:** 4 × 383 = about GHS 1,533
- **Paid:** about GHS 383
- **Arrears:** about GHS 1,150

Her loan is overdue. The committee applies the agreed GHS 50 penalty and contacts her privately. She explains a business setback, and they agree a plan: GHS 300 a month for three months while she recovers, then normal instalments. The agreement is recorded; the next payment clears the penalty first, then goes to interest and principal.

That's the process working as intended: consistent, documented and humane.
