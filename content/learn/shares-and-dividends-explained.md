In a savings group that lends, interest income belongs to the members. **Shares** are the simplest way to decide who owns what, and **dividends** are how the income is shared.

## Turning contributions into shares

Each month, part of a member's contribution may go to the welfare reserve. The rest is savings, and it buys shares at the current **share price**:

> shares bought = (contribution − welfare premium) ÷ share price

Example: a GHS 50 contribution, GHS 2 welfare premium, share price GHS 24 → 48 ÷ 24 = **2 shares**.

Members who save more own more shares — which is exactly why many funds use a [minimum contribution with optional extra](/learn/how-to-set-monthly-dues).

## Why the share price matters

Some funds raise the share price over time (say from GHS 20 to GHS 24) as the fund grows. The rule that keeps this fair:

> Contributions buy shares at the price **in force for that month**. Shares already issued are never repriced.

That way, a member who joined early and saved at the lower price isn't penalised, and nobody's history changes when the price does.

## Where dividends come from

A fund earns income mainly from:

- **interest** on member loans;
- **penalties** on late loan repayments.

After any agreed costs (and perhaps a top-up to the welfare reserve), the rest is available to distribute.

## Sharing it out: a worked example

A fund has **10,000 shares** in issue and **GHS 6,000** of income to distribute.

| Member | Shares | % of fund | Dividend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ama | 800 | 8% | GHS 480 |
| Kofi | 450 | 4.5% | GHS 270 |
| Esi | 1,200 | 12% | GHS 720 |
| Everyone else | 7,550 | 75.5% | GHS 4,530 |

Dividend per share = 6,000 ÷ 10,000 = **GHS 0.60**. Each member receives shares × 0.60, paid in cash or reinvested as more shares.

## Bonus shares

Some funds reward officials for years of service with **bonus shares**. If you do, make it explicit: record each grant with a reason, approve it openly, and never let officials grant shares to themselves. See [preventing fraud in group funds](/learn/preventing-fraud-in-group-funds).

## Keeping it straight in the books

Member savings are what the fund **owes** its members; interest income is what it has **earned**. Keeping them separate — see [double-entry bookkeeping](/learn/double-entry-bookkeeping-for-savings-groups) — is what makes a dividend calculation trustworthy.

## Common questions from members

**"I joined late — is that fair?"** Yes. Dividends follow shares, and shares follow what each member has saved. Someone who has saved for two years owns more of the fund than someone who joined last month, so they receive more of its income.

**"What if the share price goes up?"** New contributions buy fewer shares, but existing shares are never repriced. Everyone's percentage of the fund stays fair.

**"Can I take my dividend as cash?"** Many funds let members choose between cash and reinvesting it as more shares. Reinvesting grows the fund and each member's stake.

**"What happens when I leave?"** Your savings (the value of your shares, under your constitution's exit rules) are paid out, less anything you owe — for example an outstanding loan.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Paying dividends out of **welfare reserve** money — only distribute actual income.
- Paying dividends while **loans are in serious arrears** — the income may not really be there yet.
- Letting officials award themselves **bonus shares** without a members' vote.
