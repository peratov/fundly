A genuine merry-go-round is simple and honest. Everyone pays the same amount, one person receives the pot each round, and nobody ever gets more than the group put in. (See [what a merry-go-round is](/learn/what-is-a-merry-go-round) for the basics.)

Scammers borrow that familiar, trusted idea to sell something very different. Schemes circulated on social media as "blessing looms", "gifting circles", "money flowers" or "sou-sou boards" have cost people money in many countries. Here's how to tell them apart.

## The 8 warning signs

**1. You're promised more than you pay.** "Put in 500, receive 4,000." In a real circle you receive exactly what the members contribute. Any multiplication must come from someone else's pocket, usually the next wave of recruits.

**2. You must recruit to get paid.** If your payout depends on bringing in new people, it's a pyramid. Real circles have a fixed membership agreed at the start.

**3. The members are strangers.** Merry-go-rounds work because members know and can hold each other to account. A group of strangers from a Facebook or Telegram post has none of that protection.

**4. The structure is a chart.** Boards, flowers or grids with "levels" (you start on the outside and "move to the centre") are a classic pyramid design.

**5. Pressure and urgency.** "Only two spots left, pay in the next hour." Genuine groups agree terms calmly.

**6. Payments go to people you've never met.** Especially if you're told to send money to a different person each time without any record held by the group.

**7. No written rules and no record.** A legitimate circle can show who paid what and who received. See our [merry-go-round rules template](/learn/merry-go-round-rules-template) for what a real one looks like.

**8. It's called a "gift".** Calling payments gifts is often an attempt to avoid being seen as an investment or financial scheme.

## Why pyramid circles always collapse

Suppose every member must bring in eight new people to be paid. The number of people needed grows very fast: 8, then 64, then 512, then 4,096, then 32,768. By the fifth or sixth level the scheme runs out of recruits. Most people who join, typically those in the later waves, lose everything they paid. The early joiners who "got paid" did so only with the losers' money.

## Protecting your own circle

Even a real circle can be abused by a dishonest member or organiser. Simple safeguards:

- only invite people you would lend the amount to yourself;
- keep a written schedule every member can see (the [free planner](/tools/merry-go-round) generates one with a shareable link);
- pay the recipient or a dedicated group account directly, never a personal account the organiser controls alone;
- ask for proof of payment every round (see [receipts and proof of payment](/learn/receipts-and-proof-of-payment));
- put newer members later in the order.

For larger funds, our guide to [preventing fraud in group funds](/learn/preventing-fraud-in-group-funds) covers controls such as dual signatories and monthly reconciliation.

## If you've already joined

1. **Stop paying in.** Don't pay again to "unlock" your payout.
2. **Keep evidence.** Screenshot the chats, the payment confirmations and the names and numbers involved.
3. **Tell the people you invited**, even if it's awkward. It may save them money.
4. **Report it** to the police, and to your mobile money provider or bank, who may be able to block receiving accounts.

## Start a safe one instead

If what you want is disciplined saving with people you trust, run a proper circle. Our step-by-step guide to [starting a merry-go-round with friends](/learn/how-to-start-a-merry-go-round-with-friends) covers who to invite, how to set the amount and how to agree the order fairly.
