Stokvels are South Africa's version of the community savings clubs found across Africa. They are known elsewhere as [susu in Ghana](/learn/susu-ghana-guide), [chama in Kenya](/learn/chama-kenya-guide) and [esusu or ajo in Nigeria](/learn/esusu-ajo-nigeria-guide). They are a big part of household finance: millions of South Africans belong to at least one, and together they save tens of billions of rand each year.

## The main types of stokvel

**Rotating stokvel.** This is the classic model. Each member contributes a fixed amount monthly and one member receives the whole pot each month, in the same way as a [merry-go-round](/learn/what-is-a-merry-go-round).

**Savings stokvel.** Members contribute monthly into a shared account, and the total is paid out at year end, often in December for the holidays. Interest earned is shared.

**Grocery stokvel.** Members save through the year and buy groceries in bulk together, often with retailer discounts, then share the goods out before the festive season.

**Burial society.** Members pay a monthly premium, and the society pays out when a member or a covered relative dies, helping with funeral costs. This is close to the welfare cover many alumni funds offer; see [welfare packages explained](/learn/welfare-packages-explained).

**Investment stokvel.** Members pool money to invest in property, shares or a business, aiming for long-term growth rather than a regular payout.

Many groups combine two types, for example a monthly rotation plus a small burial fund.

## Typical stokvel rules

Strong stokvels usually have a written constitution covering:

- the monthly contribution and the due date;
- fines for late payment or missed meetings;
- the payout order (rotating) or payout date (savings and grocery);
- who the signatories are and how money is withdrawn;
- what happens when a member dies or leaves;
- how disputes are resolved.

Our [group fund constitution template](/learn/group-fund-constitution-template) is a good starting point to adapt.

## Banking a stokvel

Keeping money in a member's personal account is the most common cause of stokvel losses. Most major South African banks offer **dedicated stokvel accounts**. They usually need the constitution, minutes appointing signatories and the signatories' IDs, and withdrawals need more than one signatory. Our guide to [opening a group bank account](/learn/opening-a-group-bank-account) explains mandates and signatories in detail.

Joining a national stokvel association can also give access to member benefits and guidance.

## Keeping a stokvel healthy

- **Meet regularly**, even briefly online, and present the numbers each time. A simple [monthly financial report](/learn/monthly-financial-report-for-members) builds trust quickly.
- **Rotate responsibilities** so no single person controls everything.
- **Treat fines as a last resort.** They work best when the rules are clear and applied to everyone equally.
- **Watch for "stokvels" that aren't.** Schemes that promise to double your money, or that pay you only when you recruit others, are pyramid schemes, not stokvels. See [merry-go-round scams and warning signs](/learn/merry-go-round-scams-warning-signs).

## Moving your stokvel records online

Many stokvels still use a notebook and a WhatsApp group. That works until the treasurer changes, a dispute arises, or the group grows. Moving the register to shared software means every member can see their own contributions, and handovers become painless. Our guide on [moving from WhatsApp and Excel](/learn/moving-from-whatsapp-and-excel) covers how to switch without losing history.
