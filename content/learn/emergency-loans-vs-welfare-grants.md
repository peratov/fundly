A member's child is in hospital, a house floods, a parent dies. Your fund wants to help quickly. The question is whether to **lend** or **give**. Both have a place, and the best funds offer both, with clear rules about which applies when.

## Welfare grants: money that doesn't come back

A welfare grant is a benefit paid from welfare contributions, which work like pooled insurance. The member never repays it. Our guide to [welfare packages](/learn/welfare-packages-explained) covers typical benefits and amounts.

**Best for:**

- bereavement, which is almost universal in alumni and community funds;
- serious illness or hospitalisation;
- disasters (fire, flood);
- celebrations, if your fund supports weddings or births.

**Cost to the fund:** the full amount, funded from welfare contributions. It must be budgeted; see [how to set monthly dues](/learn/how-to-set-monthly-dues).

## Emergency loans: fast money that's repaid

An emergency loan is a small, fast loan with a simplified process. The member repays it, usually over a short term.

**Best for:**

- needs larger than the welfare benefit;
- urgent but non-catastrophic costs, like an unexpected school bill or a car repair needed for work;
- events outside the welfare scheme's cover.

**Cost to the fund:** only the money that isn't repaid, plus lost interest if you charge none. The capital returns to the fund.

## Designing an emergency loan product

- **Fast:** decision within 24–48 hours, approved by two officers rather than the full committee.
- **Small:** capped, for example at one month's typical income or a fixed amount like GHS 2,000.
- **Short:** repaid over 3–6 months.
- **Cheap:** 0% or a low rate.
- **Simple:** minimal paperwork, with no guarantor needed below the member's own savings.
- **One at a time:** only one emergency loan outstanding per member.

Keep it separate from normal loans in your [loan application and approval process](/learn/loan-application-and-approval-process), so it isn't stuck in the normal queue.

## A decision guide

| Situation | Grant | Emergency loan |
|---|---|---|
| Death of member or close family | Yes | Optional top-up |
| Hospitalisation | Yes, up to the benefit limit | For costs above it |
| Fire or flood at home | Yes | Often both |
| Unexpected school fees | No | Yes |
| Job loss | Sometimes, if in the scheme | Yes, cautiously |
| Business opportunity | No | No, use a normal loan |

## Protecting the fund

- **Keep reserves.** Emergency loans come from the liquidity reserve, so don't lend it all out (see [setting loan limits](/learn/setting-loan-limits-for-members)).
- **Budget welfare separately** from savings, so grants never eat into members' savings.
- **Track repayment.** Emergency loans are forgiven more often than normal loans. Handle arrears gently but consistently, and if one truly can't be repaid, account for it properly, as covered in [writing off bad loans](/learn/writing-off-bad-loans-and-provisions).
- **Report totals** (not names) monthly, so members see the fund doing its job.

## A worked example

A fund's welfare scheme pays GHS 3,000 on the death of a member's parent. Adwoa's father dies and the funeral will cost about GHS 7,000. The fund pays her the **GHS 3,000 grant** within two days. She also applies for a **GHS 2,000 emergency loan** at 0% over five months, repaid at GHS 400 a month. Two officers approve it the same afternoon because it is within her own savings. The fund's net cost is the grant, which was budgeted, plus a little interest income forgone on the loan. Adwoa gets GHS 5,000 when she needs it most.

## Getting it approved

Propose your emergency loan rules at a general meeting and add them to your [constitution](/learn/group-fund-constitution-template). Once members agree, the committee can act in hours, not weeks, when someone needs help most.
