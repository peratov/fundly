  - q: What is a savings multiple?
    a: A rule that a member can borrow up to a set multiple of their own savings, commonly two or three times. A member with GHS 2,000 saved and a 3x multiple can borrow up to GHS 6,000.
  - q: How much of the fund should be lent out?
    a: Many groups keep 20 to 30 percent in cash so they can pay welfare claims and member exits without recalling loans. The right level depends on how often you pay out.
  - q: Should officers have higher limits?
    a: No. Officers should follow exactly the same limits as everyone else, and should step out of decisions on their own loans.

Lending to members is how a savings group makes savings grow. Interest on loans flows back to savers as dividends. But lending too much, or too much to one person, is also how funds get into trouble. Clear loan limits, set in advance and applied equally, protect both. This guide builds on [how to run loans in a savings group](/learn/how-to-run-loans-in-a-savings-group).

## Layer 1: The member limit (savings multiple)

The most common rule ties borrowing to each member's own savings:

> **Maximum loan = savings balance × multiple**

A 2x or 3x multiple is typical. It rewards consistent savers, and it means part of the loan is backed by the borrower's own money, which can be held as security.

| Savings | 2x limit | 3x limit |
|---|---|---|
| GHS 1,000 | GHS 2,000 | GHS 3,000 |
| GHS 2,500 | GHS 5,000 | GHS 7,500 |
| GHS 6,000 | GHS 12,000 | GHS 18,000 |

Some funds scale the multiple with track record, for example 2x for new borrowers and 3x after two loans repaid on time. That links neatly to [fair credit scoring for members](/learn/fair-credit-scoring-for-members).

## Layer 2: The affordability check

A member might qualify on savings yet be unable to afford the repayments. A simple test is that monthly repayment should not exceed about a third of the member's regular monthly income. Ask, don't assume, and record the answer on the [loan application](/learn/loan-application-and-approval-process).

## Layer 3: The single-borrower cap

No single member should hold too much of the fund's money, however large their savings. A common cap is **10–15% of total lendable funds** per borrower. If your fund has GHS 200,000 lendable, a 10% cap means no one borrows more than GHS 20,000, whatever their savings multiple says.

This rule prevents the fund from depending on one member's fortunes.

## Layer 4: The liquidity reserve

The fund also has to pay welfare claims, member exits and operating costs. Keep a reserve that is **not** lent out. Many groups hold 20–30% of total funds in cash or the bank. Groups with frequent welfare payouts hold more.

**Worked example.** A fund has GHS 250,000 in total.
- Liquidity reserve at 25%: GHS 62,500 kept back.
- Lendable: GHS 187,500.
- Single-borrower cap at 10%: GHS 18,750.

## Layer 5: Guarantees for loans above savings

Where a loan exceeds the borrower's own savings, the excess is often covered by guarantors, who pledge part of their own savings. See [guarantors in group lending](/learn/guarantors-in-group-lending) for how to set that up.

## Putting it together

A loan is approved only up to the **lowest** of:

1. savings × multiple;
2. the affordability limit;
3. the single-borrower cap;
4. what's available after the liquidity reserve.

When funds are short, lend in order of application date, or prioritise [emergency loans](/learn/emergency-loans-vs-welfare-grants), and publish the queue so members can see it's fair.

## Review limits every year

At each AGM, review the arrears rate, the liquidity position and demand for loans. If arrears are rising, tighten the multiple or the single-borrower cap. If money sits idle, loosen them. Watch how bad debts affect dividends (see [writing off bad loans](/learn/writing-off-bad-loans-and-provisions)). Also re-check your interest method; [flat vs reducing-balance interest](/learn/flat-vs-reducing-balance-interest) shows how much it changes the real cost to borrowers.
